How to read
the comparison

The map shows where; the scatterplot shows the relationship.

Each map area represents a school district. Each scatterplot mark represents one district in the selected state and district layer. Moving right means higher median household income. Moving up means higher operating spending per student. The spending axis is logarithmic: equal vertical distances represent equal proportional changes. Every district has one mark, regardless of enrollment.

Colors use the same national reference points everywhere.

The income benchmark is the U.S. median household income of $80,734 from the 2020–2024 American Community Survey. The spending benchmark is the published FY 2024 national current spending figure of $17,619 per student. A district at or above a benchmark is on the “higher” side for that measure. These references stay fixed when you change states. [Sources 1–2]

Blue areas combine lower income with higher spending; pink areas combine higher income with lower spending. Purple means both are higher; pale purple means both are lower. “Higher” and “lower” describe a comparison with these benchmarks, not a judgment about schools.

District boundaries can overlap.

Elementary and secondary districts may cover the same residents. The default layer combines unified districts with elementary districts; a separate option combines unified districts with secondary districts. This keeps both overlapping layers from being painted at once. Unified districts can also be viewed alone. [Source 3]

What the two
measures mean

School resources: current operating spending per student

This is district spending on current public elementary and secondary school operations, using the Census Bureau’s published per-pupil measure. It includes resources funded by federal, state, and local sources. Capital spending, such as school construction, and debt service are outside this measure. It is not a measure of how much local residents alone contribute. [Source 1]

Resident income: median household income

This is the median income of all households living within a district’s boundaries, including households with no children in public schools. It is a five-year survey estimate expressed in 2024 inflation-adjusted dollars, not a single-year income census or the income of the district’s students. [Source 2]

Local funding: context for a selected district

The selection detail also reports local-source revenue per student and the local share of total revenue. These are revenue measures, separate from operating spending. Local-source revenue can include taxes, parent-government contributions, tuition and other receipts; it does not by itself measure a resident’s tax burden or a community’s willingness to fund schools. [Source 1]

Read the differences
with care

  • Income estimates have uncertainty. The district detail shows the ACS 90% margin of error when available and notes when the income interval crosses the national benchmark. Map colors follow the point estimate; they are not statistical significance tests. Triangles identify income reported as an upper or lower bound, such as $250,000 or more. [Source 2]
  • Not every district has both measures. Hatched areas lack a usable matched value for this comparison. The project contains 13,305 geographic districts; 12,960 have positive spending and enrollment plus usable income across all district types. The default national layer includes 12,577 matched districts. See the dataset metadata for coverage.
  • Reported spending can be unusual. Small enrollment, tuition arrangements, and reporting practices can affect per-student figures. Very low published values are retained and flagged in the selection detail. Use the methodology and original finance records before drawing conclusions about an outlier.
  • The dollars are not adjusted for geographic cost differences. A dollar may buy different amounts of staff time and services in different places. Grade spans and student needs also differ. The display does not adjust for those differences. [Source 1]
  • Association is a starting point for inquiry. These charts do not establish that income causes spending differences, measure school quality, or rank communities by generosity. State funding rules and student needs are useful next questions.
